Guest etiquette, from invite to thank-you

Guests can make a small show feel big. They also arrive with real lives, real reputations, and zero obligation to spend an hour with a show that has four episodes. Etiquette — actual courtesy, planned properly — is the difference between "word travels, people want to come on" and "people quietly decline your emails."

The short version: Invite in writing, prep in writing, confirm consent explicitly, listen more than you talk, cut the weakest third of a long interview without embarrassment, and finish with a thank-you note plus a real promote-their-work plan. Templates below.

The invitation email (write it before you have a guest)

An invitation that says "I'd love to have you on my podcast — let me know!" puts the entire burden on the guest. Send this instead (fill in the brackets; keep it short, three short paragraphs is generous):

Subject: Invite — [Show name], a short podcast about [topic]

Hi [Name],

[One honest sentence about why them specifically — an episode of theirs you heard, something they wrote. Specific beats flattery.]

I host [show name], a small, [20–30]-minute conversational podcast about [topic]. Episodes are audio, [flat and friendly], and listeners are mostly [who they are — honestly, even if that is "about 40 people, most of them authors"].

Would you join me for a [30]-minute call? The whole shape: [15] minutes of talking about [their thing], [5] minutes wrap-up. No script; I'll send questions at least a few days before. There's a [replaceable] tech check the day before we record, if useful.

Thank you for your time either way — and no pressure at all. [Your name], [show name], [your LLC's contact email]

What makes this work is the honest numbers (no inflated "huge listener base" — experienced guests can tell, and honesty reads as respect), the clear shape (time is respected), and the no-pressure open door.

The prep email (a few days before)

Send the guest everything on one flat, skimmable page:

  • Date, time, and local time zones (double-check; timezone math has ended friendships).
  • The link to the recording tool and a "join two minutes early" note.
  • Consent statement, in plain sentences: "By joining the call you agree we're recording audio, we may edit for length and clarity, and the recording will be published as a podcast episode with your name and one link you choose. If you'd like to review your edited portion before it publishes, just say so and we'll make time."
  • Your planned questions (5–8 max), no ambush corner: guests do their best thinking when the questions are visible in advance.
  • One line about length and timing: "[20–30] minutes of recording, hard stop at [X] because I respect your calendar."

During the interview: five rules of being a good host

  1. You talk last, and least. The guest is the content on guest episodes. If your monologue goes over a minute and a half in a single stretch, the episode has lost its point.
  2. Silence is fine. After they finish a thought, wait two full seconds before you speak. Some of the best quotes arrive in the second half of an answer.
  3. Never finish their sentences, and don't interrupt with your version of their story. Ask, then stop talking.
  4. Watch the clock quietly. If you promised 30 minutes, honor it. Running long "because the conversation was good" spends their time without asking — a one-time kindness becomes rudeness when repeated.
  5. Names and claims, live. When the guest mentions a number or a study, note it at the moment ("that's the seven-day rule, right?") and let them confirm. Live verification beats post-recording archaeology in your transcript pass.

After the recording: the courtesy loop

  1. Same day, one short note: "Thank you — recording worked, I'll send the episode the week before it publishes."
  2. Edit with respect. Cut the weakest third if it drags (Chapter 4 explains trimming between, never inside, segments). But never make the guest look or sound foolish for your content: if they stumbled, cut it. If they said something vulnerable they might regret, ask before including it.
  3. The proposed publish plan, in one email: publish date, the exact sentence you'll use to introduce them, one link they want promoted, and a "any changes?" chance to correct. This step catches 90% of the mistakes an AI transcript pass might have introduced (Names, numbers — Chapter 5's checklist runs twice: once for your notes, once against the guest's real life).
  4. Thank-you note the day it publishes, with the direct link, the one linked quote or clip, and one thing you personally learned. Specific, warm, done in three sentences. No "could you also..." paragraphs.
  5. Promote their work, not your show. Tag them where they actually exist; link the page they gave you. Guests who get real promotion come back, recommend you, and — the quiet secret of small shows — are far more likely to promote you in an honest, unhurried way.

The three invitations you should never send

  • "Can we also film this for YouTube and post the audio everywhere?" (Scope creep before the first yes.)
  • "Do you want to come on again next week? We'll figure it out later." (No plan = no respect for their time.)
  • "Happy to send you the contract we didn't tell you about!" (If your show is part of your LLC and you use written guest terms, share them in the prep email — never ambush a guest with paperwork at the record button. Plain-language consent in writing covers most small shows; this is not legal advice and your LLC's situation deserves a professional's eyes at some point.)

When someone declines

Declines are information, not insults. Reply in two sentences: "No worries at all — thanks for considering it. If [a specific future topic] ever fits, the door's open." Then close the tab. A gracious no from a busy person often becomes a yes in six months when the show has grown, and nobody forgets a non-drama decline.

A note on disputes and corrections

If a guest later says you misquoted or misattributed something — believe them first, verify second, fix third: correct the notes and the transcript, re-export the chapter timestamps if needed, and say so plainly in the corrected episode thread. Handling one correction cleanly earns more trust than twenty months of never making one.
