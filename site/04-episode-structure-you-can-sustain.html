An episode structure you can sustain

The shows that last are not the ones with the best ideas. They are the ones with a shape the host can repeat at 9 pm on a Tuesday when the day went badly. This chapter gives you that shape, plus the batch habits that make it nearly effortless to keep filling.

The short version: One skeleton, filled in tired: cold open, promise, three segments, takeaway, outro. Twenty to thirty minutes. Fill the Episode planner worksheet in ten minutes, record in one take plus pickups, and never start an episode without knowing where it ends.

Why structure beats inspiration

Structure is a gift to three versions of you:

  • Tonight-you writes the outline in ten minutes instead of staring at a blank page.
  • Recording-you never loses the thread, because the thread is on paper.
  • Editing-you cuts in minutes, because she knows where each section starts and ends.

Skip the skeleton and every episode becomes an improvisation problem — which is exactly how shows die in month three. Fake freedom ("we'll see where it goes") costs real energy every single week.

The skeleton: five parts, twenty to thirty minutes

  1. Cold open (15–45 seconds). The single most interesting sentence from the middle of the episode, spoken first, before any music or welcome. "You can record a decent episode in your bedroom with a phone and a blanket — here's exactly how." This is a trailer for the next 20 minutes. Fake cold open ("as we were saying... um... uh...") is what happens when the episode already lost its shape.
  2. Welcome and promise (30–60 seconds). Who you are in one line, what the listener will have by the end, and — for new listeners — who the show is for. "This is the Podcast Friend Starter Guide show. In the next 25 minutes you'll have a bedroom studio, a three-tool free editing kit, and a reason not to buy a mixer."
  3. Body: exactly three segments. Not two (too thin), not four (bloat), not five (a committee). Three segments, each announced before it starts: "Three things today: the room, the technique, the gear." Two rules make them work:
    • Each segment earns its spot with one deliverable: a step list, a comparison table, a rule of thumb, a number the listener could repeat. If a segment has no deliverable, it's a vibe, and locate it on the calendar later or cut it.
    • Segments should be cuttable. If running long, segment three goes in next week's episode, whole. Listeners tolerate "part one, continued" — they do not tolerate a rambling stretch that dies at minute 41.
  4. Takeaway (60–90 seconds). Say the three things again, compressed. Not a summary of everything — just what you want a listener to still know tomorrow. "Three things to remember: room first, peaks at -12 to -6, and room tone every session."
  5. Outro (30 seconds). One call to action — one — then stop. "If this helped, the worksheet is at the show page." A second action ("and leave a review, and share it, and join my newsletter, and...") is how you guarantee listeners do none of them.

Total: 20–30 minutes for a solo voice. Longer is fine for interviews (Chapter 6); longer is rarely fine for solo, because your preparation cost grows with the square of the length.

Filling the planner in ten minutes

The Episode planner worksheet asks you for seven short answers:

  1. Title that says the utility out loud. "How I record in my bedroom" beats "Episode 27."
  2. The cold-open sentence. One line. If you can't write it, the episode isn't ready to record.
  3. The promise to the listener.
  4. Three segment titles, each with its deliverable type.
  5. The takeaway list (the three things, compressed).
  6. The single call to action.
  7. Running time target.

You fill this sitting down with tea, not at the microphone. Ten minutes here saves an hour in the edit and (honestly) a few episodes' worth of shame spirals when the take goes sideways.

Records you can batch without burning out

Batching is the difference between "I do a podcast" and "the podcast does me":

  • The outline afternoon (30 minutes): fill in planners for the next 2–3 episodes. Not recording — just paper. Ideas arrive when the pressure to produce is off.
  • The recording morning (60–90 minutes): record two episodes back to back, same setup, same voice. Number 2 is nearly always better than number 1 — warm voice, in-practice mistakes already made once.
  • The edit evening (45 minutes–1.5 hours): edit both while the cuts are still fresh in your head.
  • The publishing slot (20 minutes): upload, write show notes (Chapter 5), schedule both. Publish one now, one next week. You are now one episode ahead, permanently, which is the entire secret of calm shows.

You do not need all four slots every week. With two recorded episodes banked, a slow week is just the publishing slot, and the show's heartbeat never skips.

Length, and the two-dropout traps

Keep solo shows in the 20–30 range. Two traps:

  • The hour-long escape hatch. Recordings stretch ("we just kept talking") when preparation felt thin. When an episode runs long, the cure is usually preparation, not editing discipline: write a tighter planner before you re-record.
  • The mid-topic cliffhanger. Cutting a topic in half because the take ran long leaves the listener hanging on both ends. Cut between segments, never inside them.

If an interview runs long, cut the interview open and trim the weakest third calmly. A tight 35 beats a floppy 60 every single time — and your listeners' commutes stay easy.

A shape the tired-you can still run

On a bad week (sick, deadline, brain fog), the show shrinks rather than dies. Same skeleton, shorter segments:

  • 20-second cold open of the middle takeaway.
  • 30-second promise.
  • One segment (just one, with one deliverable).
  • The takeaway, honest: "We kept this short this week on purpose."
  • Outro with the same call to action.

Call it the minimum viable episode: ten minutes, one idea, done. It is fully legitimate — no apology culture, no "sorry for the short one." A show that can shrink without breaking is a show it's almost impossible to quit accidentally (and when quitting is the right call, chapter 8 makes it a conscious decision, not a fade-out).

The episode-shape audit (do this after every five episodes)

  1. Did any episode exceed 40 minutes? Was the cause thin prep?
  2. How many cold opens were you actually proud of? (Honest number, not generous.)
  3. Which segment type do listeners finish? Check your host's play-dropoff chart once, not obsessively.
  4. Is the call to action still one thing? (Drift to "and also..." is the most common slow leak.)
  5. Do you have 2 episodes banked right now? If not, schedule the recording morning before anything else.

Write the answers in your planner file, 5 minutes, then close the spreadsheet-shaped anxiety and go outside.
