Bedroom recording on a budget

Good news: your bedroom is already a decent studio. It just needs to stop sounding like a drum. This chapter takes you from "phone in your hand" to "clean voice file on disk" without a single purchase, and then tells you which one small purchase actually matters if you keep going.

The short version: Record in a soft, quiet room; keep the mic 4–8 inches from your mouth; aim your loudest peaks around -12 to -6 dB; save audio files with careful names; then fix small problems in the edit. Gear comes last.

The three things that actually matter

In order of importance:

  1. The room. A quiet, soft room with a phone mic beats a $400 mic in a hard, echoey room. Every time. No exceptions.
  2. The technique. Consistent mic distance, steady level, decent posture, water within reach. This is what makes listeners stay.
  3. The gear. A distant third until you have recorded three episodes (see Chapter 1).

If a store tells you the microphone is the secret, remember the order: room, technique, then gear.

Making the room soft (free, today)

Hard surfaces — bare walls, desk tops, bare floors — reflect your voice back a few milliseconds late, which is the "you're underwater" sound. Fix it with what you own:

  • Record inside a closet full of hanging clothes if you can fit a chair in it. Clothes are the cheapest acoustic foam in existence.
  • No closet? Make a mini-studio: drape a heavy blanket over the back of two chairs, sit in front of it, and put the mic between you and the blanket.
  • Other free absorbers: a mattress against the wall behind you, curtains over the window, a rug between your chair and a hard floor, a full bookshelf.
  • Avoid rooms that buzz: kitchens (fridges), rooms with window A/C units, and anywhere a laptop fan can face the mic.

Pick your recording time around the room's habits: after the street calms down, before the air conditioner kicks on. Record five seconds of test audio and listen on headphones — count the noises you can hear. If the answer is "the fridge," schedule around it. That's free studio time.

Mic technique (free, always)

Whatever the microphone is — phone, laptop, earbuds, or a real mic later — these stay the same:

  • Distance: 4–8 inches from your mouth, slightly off to the side, because plosive pops ("p", "b") come straight out of your mouth, not sideways. This alone replaces a pop filter for most setups.
  • Level: peaks around -12 to -6 dB. In your recording app's meter, your loudest normal words should hit minus twelve to minus six; never touch the red. If your level is too low, get 1 inch closer or speak 10% louder — do not turn the computer volume up, which raises room noise too.
  • Drift check: keep one hand on the desk. People drift away from microphones mid-sentence, and the volume jumps around in the edit. Sit up, plant your feet, and check distance before each section.
  • Water: room-temperature, within reach. Iced water clicks, hot tea fogs a close mic. Sip between sentences, not across them.
  • The 5-second test take, every session: say "Testing, one two, [your name], it's Tuesday." Listen back once. Too quiet, too poppy, too echoey? Fix it before the real take, not after two failed 40-minute takes.

What to record with (what you already own)

  • Phone voice memo app. Voice Memos (iPhone) or a solid recorder app on Android, set to the highest quality. Mono is fine; phone mics are genuinely good. Put the phone on a folded towel rather than the hard table — desk taps travel like drums.
  • Wired earbuds with an inline mic: surprisingly clean, and consistently close to your mouth, which fixes drift automatically.
  • Laptop built-in mic only if its fan is quieter than a small dog, and only with hard headphones (any wired pair) so the computer speakers don't feed back.
  • A dynamic USB microphone — only after the three-episode rule. Recommended shape: a dynamic (not condenser) USB mic around $40–$70, on a small desk stand or boom arm. Dynamic mics hear less room echo and keyboard clatter, which makes them the right personality for bedrooms. Do not buy a condenser mic for an untreated bedroom; it hears everything, including the fridge in the next room.

Do not buy yet, no matter what any video recommends: a mixer, an audio interface, XLR everything, shockmounts, or acoustic foam panels for a room you also sleep in. Those come after months, and only if a real need shows up.

File habits (the boring 10% that saves you)

Give each recording one name and one home on the day you record it. No exceptions. Format:

  • ep01_show-promise_2026-10-30_raw.wav — the untouched original, kept forever.
  • Finished mixes get their own copy: ep01_show-promise_2026-10-30_final.mp3.

One folder tree, kept boring on purpose: raw takes, finished episodes, notes and worksheets. Back that folder up in two places — a second drive plus a cloud account, or two drives — and test the backup once a month by opening one file from it.

Three habits that pay off in the edit (next chapter):

  • Record 15 seconds of room tone (everyone sitting silent) at the start of every session. Editing software uses it to patch gaps so cuts sound like nothing happened.
  • Note your mic setup in one line per session ("closet, phone, 6 inches") so episode 30 sounds like episode 1.
  • Save a fresh copy before every big edit; keep the raw original untouched forever.

The one small purchase that earns its keep

After three real episodes, if the show has earned it, buy:

  1. A dynamic USB microphone (about $40–$70). This fixes what beginners actually suffer from: room sound, plosives, and drifting distance. Skip condenser mics and every bundle with a mixer until a real need appears.

Wired headphones you already own count as gear. If you must buy, a basic $20 wired pair is plenty — you need to hear the truth, not the bass.

Everything past that one microphone — interfaces, XLR cables, cloud lifters, things with superhero names — is later money, paid for by you joyfully once the show has proven itself, or by listeners if it grows that way. Not before.

The pre-recording checklist

Before every session, run this by hand until it is automatic:

  1. Mic within 4–8 inches, slightly off-axis from your mouth.
  2. Something soft within 3 feet of where your voice goes.
  3. Fridge, A/C, fans, and phone notifications: off or on notice.
  4. Ten-second room tone recorded.
  5. Test take: peaks around -12 to -6 dB, no red.
  6. Water within reach, notes on paper so clicking keyboards stay off-mic.

Print it or keep it in your notes app. This card is worth more than any microphone.
