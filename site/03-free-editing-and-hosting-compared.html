Free editing and hosting, compared

This chapter walks the two shopping-lists every new podcaster faces — editing software and a hosting company — and covers both with free options compared side by side. Then it makes one simple recommendation path so you can stop researching and start publishing.

The short version: Editing: use Audacity (free, runs on everything) or GarageBand if you have a Mac. Hosting: start on a free tier of a real podcast host — Spotify for Creators is the easiest true free-forever option; Buzzsprout and Podbean have limited free tiers — and never let anyone else own your RSS feed.

Editing software, compared

You need four things from an editor: cut out mistakes, level the volume, export MP3, and not fight you. That's it. Everything else is optional.

Tool Price Platforms Best for Watch out for
Audacity Free, open source Windows, Mac, Linux The default choice. Noise reduction, loudness normalization, MP3 export Old-fashioned interface; menus instead of pretty buttons
GarageBand Free with Apple hardware Mac Mac users who want the least friction Podcast features have been simplified over the years; keep your templates
ocenaudio Free Windows, Mac, Linux Quick, simple edits on one file Fewer advanced features for music/multi-track work
Reaper $60 license with an unlimited free-continue trial Windows, Mac, Linux People who want a real DAW at a fair price The trial nags politely but keeps working; overkill for most solo shows
DaVinci Resolve Fairlight Free Windows, Mac, Linux People already editing video there Heavy download; more interface than you need
In-browser AI tools (various) Freemium Any browser Auto-leveling and rough cuts from a text transcript Free tiers often watermark, watermark audio, or limit length; check export rights, and prefer local tools for private material

Recommendation: Audacity on Windows/Linux, GarageBand on Mac — and learn exactly four skills: cut silence, amplify/normalize, noise reduction with your room tone, and export at 128+ kbps. Do this once and you never need another tutorial again. Here they are for Audacity in ten lines:

  1. Import your raw WAV (File > Import).
  2. Select 5–10 seconds of room tone at the top and Effect > Noise Removal and Repair > Noise Reduction > "Get Noise Profile."
  3. Select all, apply Noise Reduction with the defaults.
  4. Listen for mistakes: rambling, repeated takes, "um", double breaths, taps.
  5. Select and delete each mistake; taper cuts two seconds after the last word stops, not the exact instant — cutting too tight sounds nervous.
  6. Select all, Effect > Volume and Compression > Loudness Normalization, target -16 LUFS for mono voice.
  7. normalized already? Skip it; the host does it. (See below.)
  8. Extraneous clicks: zoom in, they're visible as spikes; delete.
  9. File > Export > MP3, 128 kbps, mono, your careful file name from Chapter 2.
  10. Listen to the first and last three minutes before publishing. Every time.

The one contraction that matters

Text-based editing tools (edit audio by editing its transcript) are the current shiny thing. They are genuinely fast, and the free tiers are usually enough for a solo start — but read the fine print before you trust one: what happens to your uploads, whether the export is real MP3 or a preview, and what the "free" plan will feel like after month three. If your material is private or tied to your LLC's business, the local-first answer (Audacity + local transcription in Chapter 5) keeps everything on your own disk.

Hosting, compared

Your audio files live somewhere that generates an RSS feed — that feed is what Apple, Spotify, YouTube Music, and every other app subscribe to. "Podcast hosting" means "files plus feed," and the single most important line in any hosting comparison is this: who owns the feed, and can you take it with you?

Host Free tier What it really is The catch
Spotify for Creators Unlimited, free forever True free hosting (the old Anchor) Your files live on Spotify's system; you can move the feed out, but downloads are easiest to re-upload yourself
Buzzsprout 2 hours/month, episodes removed after 90 days Polished starter host; great stats and support Old episodes vanish from the free tier — your archive slowly disappears
Podbean 5 hours total Straightforward free tier Storage-limited; live episodes accumulate fast in a news-style show
RedCircle Free tier Good monetization options later Smaller company; check current limits before committing
RSS.com Free tier (limits apply) Clean interface Free tier limits storage; read before episode 40
Transistor, Libsyn, Blubrry Paid, ~$12–$19+/month Professional standard, serious stats Costs money every month — which is fine later

A quick honesty note on comparisons: free tiers change their numbers every year, and this guide can't update itself. Before you commit, check two things on the host's own pricing page today: (1) storage limits and episode-expiry on the free tier, and (2) whether the free tier lets you set your own podcast name and get a portable RSS feed URL you control. If both are "yes," the comparison above is a starting map, not a verdict.

Recommendation path:

  1. Zero dollars: Spotify for Creators, unlimited free uploads, and it distributes to Apple Podcasts, YouTube Music, and more via your feed. This gets you a real show in a real app today.
  2. The day the show earns it or your archive matters: move to a paid host (Transistor, RSS.com paid, Libsyn — whichever you trust most after reading their current terms) and use their import tool to take your feed along. The move takes an afternoon and keeps every subscriber if the feed stays yours.

Three rules that keep you safe

  1. Own the feed. The RSS feed URL is the show's address. If the host lets you export it or point it anywhere (a domain redirect is best), you can switch hosts later and nobody notices except your stats page. If they don't offer export, that's a red flag no matter how good the free tier is.
  2. Nobody takes your archive without a fight. Keep a copy of every published MP3 and your show notes in your own folder tree (Chapter 2). Hosts have deplatformed shows by accident before; your backups make the accident a shrug.
  3. Free music means free-license music. For intro/outro sound, use free-license libraries (check exact license terms; some require credit in your episode notes), a cheap but capable home recording of your own, or no music at all — a well-edited voice opening is a perfectly professional choice. Never lift a pop song "just this once." Companies enforce, and your LLC's name is on the show.

What about video podcasts?

Recording video is optional. If you want the YouTube crowd, the calmest path is: audio-first show, and paste a waveform image or clip into a video export later. Trying to be simultaneously video-first and audio-first in month one is how a small show becomes two full jobs. (Your companion manual, the audiobook-recording handbook, covers the recording-performance side if you ever book narration-style video.)

Setup checklist once you pick a host

  1. Show name, one-sentence promise (from your worksheet) as the description.
  2. Cover art at 3000x3000 px (a free editor makes this in an evening).
  3. An email address that lives in the feed (your LLC's contact, not your personal one).
  4. Categories: one primary ("Education" or "Business"), one secondary.
  5. Explicit tag: No, unless your show judges otherwise.
  6. Publish episode 1, then check it in a real app (Apple Podcasts search takes a few days; Spotify, usually hours).
