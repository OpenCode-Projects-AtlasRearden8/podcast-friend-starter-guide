# What to say before you buy anything

Almost everyone starts a podcast by shopping. They buy a microphone, then a mixer, then discover they have nothing to say with any of it. This chapter flips the order: first you figure out *what you will say* — for free, on paper — and only then do you spend anything.

> **The short version:** Write your show's one-sentence promise, list ten episode ideas, and record three episodes on your phone before you spend a single dollar. Fill out the [Show on a page worksheet](worksheets/show-on-a-page.md) as you go.

## The one-sentence promise

Every sustainable show has a promise the listener can repeat after one episode. Here is the test: a friend of your ideal listener asks, "What's that podcast about?" — can you answer in one sentence, without rambling?

Bad answers are vague on purpose:

- "It's about AI." (About isn't a promise.)
- "We talk about everything in publishing." (Fog.)
- "It's me, thinking out loud." (A diary is fine, but then the promise is a specific person's head, and that person has to be interesting on schedule.)

Good answers name the listener, the value, and the shape:

- "Every other week, a 20-minute episode that helps self-published authors use one AI tool without breaking their book."
- "A nurse-turned-AI-author explains agents, MCP, and local AI like a friend would — plain English, no jargon walls."

Notice what the good ones have: a **who** (self-published authors), a **what** (one AI tool per episode), and a **how often and how long** (every other week, 20 minutes). Write yours before anything else. It goes in slot one of the worksheet.

## The ten-episode test

On paper — a worksheet, a napkin, whatever — list ten episodes you could record *this month* with no guests, no equipment, and no research wall in front of them. Rules:

1. Each idea must be something you could already talk about for ten minutes today.
2. At least three of them must be evergreen — still useful in two years. (Your launch announcement, your origin story, your rant about that one conference: those age like milk. Mix in advice episodes that always hold.)
3. If you cannot get to ten, that is not a failure of creativity — it's information. Either narrow the topic (ten ideas about "AI for self-published authors" beats ten ideas about "technology"), or widen it slightly until ten arrive.

If you find ten ideas and still feel a pull to record, keep reading. If you cannot find ten, the honest next step is a newsletter or a blog, not a microphone. Both cost less, your audience still finds you, and you can start a show later once ten ideas show up.

## Say it before you buy it

The money question is not "which microphone?" It's "which microphone *deserves to be bought by a person who has recorded three episodes?*" The three-episode rule:

- **Episode 1:** record with anything — a phone, a laptop, the earbuds that came with your phone. The goal is to hear yourself say your one-sentence promise out loud and find out what breaks (usually: you talk too fast, the room echoes, you say "um" a lot).
- **Episode 2:** record again a week later, applying what embarrassed you in episode 1.
- **Episode 3:** record a third time. Only now — after three honest attempts — do you get to think about spending money, and only on the one thing that actually annoyed you. In most bedrooms that is a dynamic USB microphone plus a small desk stand, which Chapter 2 covers for less than the cost of a nice dinner.

This rule is not about saving pennies. It's about making sure the gear purchases follow the show, not the other way around. Gear bought ahead of three episodes becomes guilt with a receipt.

## What your LLC needs to know (before, not after)

If the show lives under your one-person LLC, settle these four things on day one — they cost nothing now, and they prevent expensive confusion later:

1. **Who owns it.** The show, its name, and its feed should belong to the LLC or to you — pick one, write it on the worksheet, done. Don't leave it ambiguous for three years.
2. **What it's for.** "Educational audio show about X." That phrase keeps you honest when someone offers a partnership that doesn't fit.
3. **What it will never do.** Most solo shows benefit from writing down "we never sell listener data, we never take unsigned sponsorships." It's also a good sentence to look at when a weird email arrives.
4. **Where records live.** One folder, backed up twice — Chapter 2 sets up the folder structure. Note on the worksheet where it lives.

One caution, said plainly: if you ever discuss medical, legal, or financial topics, keep the *educational* boundary in every episode — a nine-second disclaimer at the top does the job ("I'm a nurse and an author, not *your* nurse"). That is not legal advice, and neither is this guide; it's the same friend's voice saying: keep it educational, keep your receipts, and talk to a professional about your specific situation.

## Name and cover art (back-of-envelope only)

You do not need a name until episode 3 exists, but a placeholder helps you write the promise. Ground rules that cost nothing now:

- A name you can say once and have spelled correctly over the phone. If you have to spell it twice, it will cost you listeners.
- Check that the name isn't already a show in your topic before you fall in love with it (a search of your podcast app's directory takes two minutes).
- The cover image can wait until launch week: 3000x3000 pixels, readable at thumbnail size. Make it in a free editor, or pay a designer after episode 10 — not before. That is the order that keeps the budget alive.

## Your three pre-launch tasks

1. Fill out the [Show on a page worksheet](worksheets/show-on-a-page.md) — promise, ten ideas, LLC notes.
2. Record three practice episodes with what you own (the next chapter tells you how).
3. Set your launch number: how many episodes exist before you tell anyone the show exists. Three is plenty. Ten is overkill, and delaying to ten is how shows never launch. Zero gets ignored on day one, so don't launch with one either.
